    What can actually be measured

    Before promising a benchmark, be honest about what is obtainable. LinkedIn blocks automated collection of its pages, and the numbers that matter most are visible only to page administrators. This is the single thing that determines whether the measurement half of this project is real or theatre.

    Have it

    the community's published content — event pages, recaps and public docs are already out there. That is the raw material the engine runs on, and it needs nobody's permission.
    Have it
    Public post content and cadence — review a small set of similar public community pages. Record dates, themes and visible engagement; no competitor data has been collected for this comparison.

    Must ask

    the community's own LinkedIn analytics — impressions, reach, engagement rate, click-through, follower growth and follower demographics. Page-admin access is required; a Page Analyst seat or a CSV export of the last twelve months would provide this data.
    Must ask
    Website and search performance — Google Search Console and GA4. Given how much content the community is publishing, this is probably where the real story is, and it is the number organisers will care about more than likes.
    Must ask
    Whether social traffic becomes enquiries — the only metric that ends the argument. Needs the CRM or the enquiry inbox, even at the level of "where did you hear about us".

    Cannot

    Other pages' impressions, reach or click-through — these are not available from their public posts. Keep any comparison to verified public measures such as reactions, comments, reposts, cadence and follower count.
    Cannot
    Historical data the community never had — LinkedIn's page analytics window is limited. Pull the export early; what is not exported now is gone.

    Public monitoring focus

    Pick a small set of similar public community pages to watch. Confirm the exact pages before collecting or comparing posts. This focus does not establish anyone's legal identity, operating status or competitive threat.

    Once verified public posts are collected, compare cadence, themes, median reactions per post and comment-to-reaction ratio. Include reposts and follower counts where visible, and label the collection period and any follower normalisation. No live competitor figures or comparison are available yet.

    Set the expectation before you set the benchmark. Start with the community's own baseline. Public engagement can vary sharply between posts, especially when the sample is small. Use the first 60 to 90 days to establish a repeatable measurement process before drawing conclusions from any comparison set.

    What to collect in week one

    1. Twelve months of LinkedIn page analytics, exported to CSV, plus the post-level export.
    2. Follower count and the follower-demographics breakdown, saved as of today.
    3. Every post from the last twelve months with its date, theme and public engagement, classified into content types.
    4. After confirming the public profiles, classify similar community pages on the same terms.
    5. Search Console: top pages, queries and click-through for the news section.
    6. A single agreed definition of engagement rate, written down, so nobody moves the goalposts later.

    The plan

    Organisers want fewer, stronger pieces for the community, led by people who actually run the meetups and grounded in what members are building. Talks, recaps and relevant events should shape the content programme. The stages below are a proposed approach, not an agreed schedule or budget.

    The issue is how the community presents and shares what it already knows. There is source material, but the current social output does not convince organisers that this knowledge is reaching the right members. This plan focuses on making approved information clear, credible and useful across content formats, then checking whether it reaches the intended audience.

    The three gaps to address

    1. Presentation. Turn genuine community expertise into polished, accessible material without losing accuracy or the founder's voice.
    2. Distribution. Put each piece in front of its intended audience, including through content planned around relevant trade fairs.
    3. Measurement. Check whether the content earns useful attention, website visits and commercial enquiries, using data that is actually available.

    Stage 1 — establish the approach

    1. Select one genuine technical subject

      Choose a topic from the community's source material and define the intended audience with organisers.

    2. Develop one polished example

      Prepare a technical LinkedIn post and, if the subject suits it, a short video concept. Check every claim against the source.

    3. Get organiser feedback

      Review technical accuracy, clarity and voice before deciding which formats are worth developing further.

    4. Review existing evidence

      Assess available source material and performance data; note what additional access or tracking would be needed.

    Stage 2 — build a focused content programme

    1. Develop a small number of strong pieces

      Prioritise useful technical explainers, video concepts and white-paper opportunities over a fixed posting volume.

    2. Plan around relevant trade fairs

      Identify suitable events with organisers, then consider content before, during and after each one.

    3. Set an organiser-led review process

      Agree how technical claims, wording and formats are checked and approved before publication.

    Stage 3 — make it repeatable and measurable

    1. Reuse approved technical information

      Establish a practical way to adapt reviewed material into posts, videos and longer explainers, with approval before publication.

    2. Connect content to outcomes

      Where data permits, relate content engagement to useful website visits and relevant commercial enquiries.

    3. Improve from evidence

      Use actual results and organiser feedback to decide which subjects and formats deserve further work.

    4. Automate only where useful

      Consider selective support for drafting or reporting once the review process works and the value is clear.

    What to measure

    MetricWhy it mattersData needed
    Relevant audience responseComments and shares from the intended audience show whether a piece prompts useful interestPublic activity; audience review
    Technical content engagementCompare response to subjects and formats, including video and longer technical piecesSocial and website analytics access
    Useful website visitsSee whether interested readers reach and use the related technical materialWebsite analytics; tagged links
    Qualified enquiriesLook for relevant commercial conversations linked to a piece or eventEnquiry records; attribution

    Public engagement can be reviewed now. Reliable reach, website and enquiry measures depend on access to the relevant accounts and, for attribution, additional tracking. Agree a baseline before setting targets; do not infer commercial impact from posting volume alone.

    Start with the example, then decide what to repeat. One carefully reviewed piece will show whether the subject, format and technical depth suit the intended audience. Use that example and the available evidence to set priorities before committing to a wider programme.
